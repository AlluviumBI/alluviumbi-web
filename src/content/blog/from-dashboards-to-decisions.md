Most executive teams do not have a dashboard shortage. They have a decision shortage.

A decade of self-service BI produced more pages, more KPIs, and more “single views of the truth” than any leadership meeting can absorb. When every function publishes a scorecard, the operating cadence still runs on slide decks and side conversations. That is a design failure, not a tooling failure. We optimized for producing visuals, not for the questions a CEO, CFO, or COO must answer this week.

## Clarity beats inventory

A useful analytics program is small on purpose. It names the few decisions that move the P&L, names the owner of each, and traces those decisions back to trusted measures. Everything else is a working report for a specialist, not a slide for the leadership pack.

That is first-principles work, and it is unfashionable. It does not start with a Copilot demo. It starts with one question: *What would we do differently on Tuesday if this number were trustworthy by Monday?*

## Where practical AI belongs

AI earns a seat when it shortens the time to insight on those same decisions. That can mean a narrative on why a margin line moved, an anomaly flagged on a cash forecast, or a forecast the controller can challenge. It does not earn a seat as a second dashboard factory. Copilots that generate more pages faster recreate the original problem with better branding.

Power BI can host the disciplined semantic model or the noisy canvas. The difference is governance, modeling, and the willingness to retire reports. Advisory work is often the act of saying no, then automating the yes.

## What to ask this week

If you lead a mid-sized company, you do not need a transformation program to test this. Ask three questions:

1. Which three decisions are still waiting on a spreadsheet?
2. Who is allowed to change the definition of those measures?
3. What would we stop producing if those three were reliable?
