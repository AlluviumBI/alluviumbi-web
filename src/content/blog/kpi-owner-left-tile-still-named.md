The scorecard still looks live and the tile still refreshes. The name under the metric belongs to someone who left six months ago.

Nobody updated the owner field or reassigned the follow-up, so the room still acts as if that person owns the outcome. They do not. The tile is a memorial, not a management control.

![Black-and-white mountain peaks rising above a sea of valley fog, rocky ridge in the foreground](/blog/kpi-owner-left-tile-still-named-hero.jpg)

## Orphaned tiles are an ownership failure

Mid-market companies treat KPI ownership as a caption. Put a name under the number, ship the app, and call governance done.

That works until the person changes roles, leaves the company, or moves to another plant. The semantic model keeps refreshing and the app keeps distributing, but the accountability line goes quiet.

Executives notice when a red variance has no owner in the room. They notice when two leaders each assume the other still owns the metric, or when the only person who could explain the definition is gone and the tile still carries their name like a badge of trust.

This is close kin to [measures nobody can explain](/blog/measures-nobody-can-explain). A measure without a living owner is not governed. It is inherited folklore with a card visual.

It also connects to [Power BI project has no owner](/blog/power-bi-project-has-no-owner). Projects without owners stall and KPIs without owners drift. Both fail the same way: the artifact outlives the accountability.

Orphaned ownership is easy to miss in a demo. The page looks finished, the colors work, and the as-of timestamp is fresh. Nothing on screen says the named person cannot take the action the meeting is about to assign.

## Why names stick after people leave

Ownership was never a process. It was a one-time label added during the build.

HR and IT offboarding revoked mailbox and app access, but never touched scorecard metadata, measure descriptions, or the RACI behind the executive pack.

The scorecard was treated as a report rather than an operating system. Reports can be stale. Operating systems need current roles.

Teams are also afraid of breaking the visual. Changing an owner field feels cosmetic until the next escalation, and cosmetic work loses to firefighting every time.

Often the real owner is informal. Everyone “knows” who watches margin, but nobody wrote it down where the pack lives. When the informal owner leaves, the informal network leaves too.

Reorgs make it worse. Titles change and dotted lines appear, while the tile still shows last year’s org chart in miniature.

## The costs of orphaned KPI ownership

1. **Escalations bounce.** A bad number lands in the weekly review and the named owner is gone. The room spends ten minutes finding a substitute, and the decision waits a cycle.

2. **Definitions freeze in the wrong person’s head.** The departed owner carried the edge cases, like returns, intercompany, and partial periods. The model still calculates, but nobody can defend it under pressure.

3. **Shadow owners appear without a mandate.** An analyst quietly becomes the person who “looks at it” without any authority to change the business process. That is the same failure as [the analyst who became the gateway](/blog/the-analyst-who-became-the-gateway).

4. **Incentives detach from the scorecard.** Comp and performance conversations still reference the tile, but the person paid for the outcome is not the name on the page. Trust in the pack erodes.

5. **Retirement never happens.** Orphaned tiles pile up because nobody claims them, so nobody kills them. [Unused reports are a governance smell](/blog/unused-reports-are-a-governance-smell) for the same reason: no owner means no sunset.

6. **New leaders inherit ghosts.** A new VP opens the app and sees predecessors everywhere, with no way to tell ceremonial names from real ones. Onboarding turns into archaeology.

7. **Audit conversations get awkward.** When finance asks who owns a control metric, “the person who left” is not an answer. Documentation that lists departed staff is worse than a blank field.

8. **You fund a living system with dead accountability.** Capacity, gateways, and certified datasets keep running, while the management layer meant to use them points at empty chairs.

## How to fix it: make KPI ownership a living roster

1. **Inventory every executive tile with a named owner.** Cover app pages, scorecards, pinned metrics, and the measures behind them. If a named owner cannot attend the meeting, flag it this week.

2. **Require a role, not just a person.** The owner should resolve to a seat, such as Plant Controller, VP Ops, or Demand Planning Lead. People change and roles persist. Track the current incumbent separately and keep that map up to date.

3. **Put ownership in the semantic model and the app, not a slide footnote.** Measure descriptions, certified dataset docs, and scorecard metadata should agree. If [the semantic model is the product](/blog/semantic-model-is-the-product), ownership is part of the product.

4. **Wire offboarding to analytics.** When HR closes a role, trigger a workspace and scorecard review. Removing access is not enough. Reassign or retire every KPI that person owned before the goodbye lunch is forgotten.

5. **Name a backup before vacation and before resignation.** Every metric needs a primary and coverage, the same discipline you need for workspace admin. If only one person can speak to a metric, you do not have an owner. You have a bus factor.

6. **Give owners a job, not a label.** Owning a KPI means confirming the definition still matches the business, escalating when thresholds break, and approving or rejecting proposed measure changes. [Who can change a measure](/blog/who-can-change-a-measure) only works when the owner is active in the process.

7. **Retire tiles nobody will claim.** If no leader will put their name on a metric after a reorg, kill the tile on a set date. Follow the pattern in [how to retire a dashboard](/blog/how-to-retire-a-dashboard): an owner for the retirement, a replacement if any, and a calendar date.

8. **Review ownership quarterly as part of the operating cadence.** Make it part of the management review, not a data steward chore. Names on tiles should match the people who will be asked questions in the room.

9. **Publish a one-page roster for the executive pack.** List the metric, a definition link, the primary owner role, the backup, and the last confirmed date. Keep it boring. Boring is how accountability survives turnover.

## What good looks like

Every tile in the pack has a living owner and a backup. Offboarding updates the roster the same week someone leaves, and definitions survive because ownership survived. When a variance turns red, the room already knows who speaks first, and the name on the tile can raise a hand.

New leaders inherit a short roster that matches the org chart they just joined, not a wall of ghosts. Analysts stop being unofficial owners of metrics they cannot change. Finance can answer an audit question without digging through last year’s email. The scorecard is a management instrument again, not a yearbook of people who used to care.

## Executive takeaway

A departed name under a live KPI is not a small housekeeping miss. It is a false signal that someone is accountable for the number.

Reassign by role, cover for absence, and retire what nobody will own. Then the scorecard stops memorializing people and starts managing outcomes.
