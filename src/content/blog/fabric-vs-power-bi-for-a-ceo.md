The vendor conversation is a feature list. The CEO conversation is something else.

You are not choosing a prettier canvas. You are choosing whether the company will run one analytics platform or keep a pile of reports that happen to live in Microsoft’s stack.

![Black-and-white confluence of two rivers that have not mixed](/blog/fabric-vs-power-bi-for-a-ceo-hero.jpg)

## The decision is platform and ownership, not a bake-off

Power BI already reports. Fabric is a broader platform conversation about where data lands, who owns the lake, what capacity you fund, and whether IT and finance share one operating model.

Those are different questions. Mixing them is how mid-market companies buy a platform they do not operate, or refuse a conversation they actually need.

A CEO should not sit through a demo of every pane. A CEO should decide three things: what the system of record for analytic data is, who owns the semantic product, and what “enough” looks like for the next four quarters.

If you cannot answer those, a new SKU will not save you. It will host the same pile.

Capabilities change. The operating question does not: one platform with named owners, or a catalog of files with no product.

## When Power BI is enough

Most mid-market companies do not have a platform shortage. They have an ownership shortage. Power BI is enough when the following hold.

You have a small number of source systems, such as ERP, CRM, and a warehouse or two. The grain is known, and finance can name the official measures.

You can fund a [semantic model as the product](/blog/semantic-model-is-the-product) and keep reports as brochures. Refresh is a job with an owner, not gateway folklore.

Leadership uses a short certified set and the rest is sandbox. That is the sprawl rule in [Dashboard Sprawl Is a Tax](/blog/dashboard-sprawl-is-a-tax).

The argument in the ELT is about definitions, not plumbing. “What is margin” is a steward problem, not a reason to stand up a lakehouse.

If those conditions hold, expanding the estate is a governance and delivery job, not a platform replacement. Otherwise you will waste a year comparing architectures while the pack is still a paste.

Power BI as a reporting layer on agreed sources is a valid end state. Treat it as one, and do not apologize for it in a board deck.

## When the lakehouse and capacity conversation is real

Sometimes Power BI is the brochure on top of a mess you can no longer paper over. The conversation is real when the following are true.

The same facts are rebuilt in three extracts because there is no shared landing zone. Every report is a private warehouse, and analysts spend the week moving data.

IT cannot tell you where “the” customer table lives because there are five and none is certified. Power BI is being asked to be a data platform it was never meant to be on its own.

Refresh and scale are constrained by how you copy data, not by how you draw a page. You are paying for capacity to reprocess the same chaos faster.

Multiple domains need the same grain and cannot share a model until the data sits in one governed place, so manufacturing, finance, and commercial keep shipping twins.

You have named a data owner who is ready to run a platform, not just a workspace. A lake without an owner is a more expensive pile.

Notice what is not on that list: a competitor bought something, a slide said “modern,” or a team wants a new toy. Those are not CEO tests.

Capacity is a funding decision you make after you know the product. It is not a strategy. Buying more horsepower for unowned models is how you scale sprawl.

## The costs of treating this as a feature bake-off

1. **You fund a platform nobody operates.** Licenses land and workspaces multiply while the operating model stays “email the analyst.” You bought a new place to put the same reports.

2. **IT and finance fight the wrong fight.** IT wants architecture and finance wants the close pack, but neither owns the semantic product. The bake-off becomes a proxy war and delivery stalls. See [analytics programs fail in delivery](/blog/analytics-programs-fail-in-delivery).

3. **The pile survives the migration.** Every file gets a new home and nothing is retired. You paid a transformation tax to relocate [dashboard sprawl](/blog/dashboard-sprawl-is-a-tax).

4. **Definitions still have two publishers.** A lake does not pick a steward. If sales and finance both still publish revenue, you moved the slogan. [Single source of truth is a decision](/blog/single-source-of-truth-is-a-decision), not a SKU.

5. **The CEO calendar fills with plumbing.** You get asked to referee storage modes and workspace types. That is staff work. Your job is to know whether one product exists and who is accountable when the number moves.

6. **Opportunity cost hits the P&L first.** While the bake-off runs, month-end is still a scavenger hunt and inventory is still late. The [reporting drag at close](/blog/why-month-end-still-takes-a-week) does not pause for architecture.

## How to decide in 90 days

You do not need a two-year “future state.” You need a written choice and a funded owner.

1. **Write the operating question on one page.** Are we running one analytics platform with named data products, or a reporting tool on top of departmental extracts? If the room cannot pick, stop the vendor meetings.

2. **Inventory the pile before you inventory features.** Count the certified models and the twins, and find out who owns refresh. If you cannot list that, you are not ready to expand the platform. You are ready to clean.

3. **Name the product owner and the platform owner.** They are different seats. The business steward owns measures and grain, and IT or a data lead owns landing, capacity, and reliability. One person wearing both hats is how neither job gets done. Project ownership is a sibling problem, covered in [why your Power BI project has no owner](/blog/power-bi-project-has-no-owner).

4. **Set “enough” for four quarters.** If Power BI plus a governed warehouse already feeds the pack, say so, and fund the model, the steward, and the retire list. If landing is genuinely broken, scope the lakehouse conversation as a data-product program, not a reporting rewrite.

5. **Do not price-shop SKUs in the ELT.** Capacity and licensing belong in a finance-and-IT memo after the operating model is chosen, so executives see cost as a consequence of ownership rather than a feature matrix. Invented list prices in a meeting are a distraction. Get current numbers from your Microsoft agreement, not from a blog.

6. **Tie the choice to decisions, not demos.** Ask what changes on Tuesday if this is one platform, and which three metrics move first. If the answer is “we will be modern,” you have a slogan, not a decision. A [Data & AI Strategy Roadmap](/analytics-ai-strategy-roadmap) is where those decisions get written down.

## What good looks like

The CEO can say in one sentence whether the company runs a reporting layer or a data platform.

Finance knows which model is official, IT knows which landing zone is funded, and analysts know the sandbox from the product.

A vendor meeting has an agenda covering ownership, grain, refresh SLA, and the retire list. It does not include a two-hour tour of every capability.

You still grow, because the product is trusted, not because a catalog got longer.
