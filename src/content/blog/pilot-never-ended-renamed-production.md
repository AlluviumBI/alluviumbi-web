The pilot was supposed to last six weeks. It answered one question for one forum, and people clapped.

Then the workspace was renamed Production and the app was shared broadly. The temporary measures stayed temporary, the temporary owners stayed informal, and the temporary data quality checks never arrived. You did not finish a pilot. You scaled an experiment and called it done.

![Black-and-white warehouse aisle with tall metal racks and shrink-wrapped pallets under overhead lights](/blog/pilot-never-ended-renamed-production-hero.jpg)

## Renaming is not hardening

Pilots are allowed to be narrow, fragile, and held together by heroes. Production is not.

When companies skip the hardening step, they carry pilot habits to estate scale: a single admin, undocumented measures, happy-path refresh, no retirement plan, and a backlog that is mostly “one more page.”

This is how [analytics programs fail in delivery](/blog/analytics-programs-fail-in-delivery). They do not fail at the vision slide. They fail the moment someone swaps the label and drops the project discipline.

It also explains why the [first 90 days of a Power BI program](/blog/first-90-days-of-a-power-bi-program) matter. The pilot exit criteria belong in those days. Without them, day ninety is just a louder pilot.

[Proof of value in 30 days](/blog/proof-of-value-in-30-days) is useful, but proof of value is not a production operating model. Confusing the two is expensive.

## How you can tell you are still in pilot mode

There is no written definition of done for production covering ownership, coverage, quality gates, certification, support hours, and retirement rules.

The original scope question still dominates, while twelve adjacent questions got bolted on without a model redesign.

Only one person can publish safely, so a vacation is an outage risk.

Refresh monitoring only checks for green. Wrong-but-successful loads still reach executives.

Measures carry temporary names and temporary logic, and nobody scheduled the cleanup.

The steering committee still [reviews screenshots](/blog/steering-committee-still-reviews-screenshots) of the “production” app. The management cadence never left pilot theater.

Success is still described as “we delivered Power BI,” not “these five decisions now run on a trusted model every week.”

## The costs of a pilot wearing a production badge

1. **Risk scales faster than capability.** More users hit unfinished edges and incidents multiply. Trust drops just as adoption metrics rise.

2. **Hidden work becomes the real run cost.** Heroes keep the experiment alive after hours. The budget never shows the labor that stands in for missing process.

3. **Roadmaps lie.** Leadership funds enhancements on a platform that never closed its foundational gaps, and every new page inherits the unfinished base.

4. **Governance arrives as blame.** When something breaks in “production,” people ask why controls were missing. The honest answer, that it was still a pilot, sounds like an excuse.

5. **Sibling pilots spawn.** Other teams copy the renamed experiment, and sprawl becomes policy by example. [Dashboard sprawl is a tax](/blog/dashboard-sprawl-is-a-tax) shows up as a portfolio of unfinished twins.

6. **Vendors and internal teams talk past each other.** One side thinks go-live happened. The other still sees prototype debt. Both are right under different definitions of done.

7. **Executives over-read early wins.** A pilot that answered one question gets asked to run the company. It cannot, and the disappointment lands on the technology brand instead of the missing exit criteria.

8. **You cannot retire what you never productized.** Without owners and acceptance criteria, nothing graduates and nothing dies. The estate fills up with permanent drafts.

## How to fix it: close the pilot or demote the label

1. **Write production exit criteria now, even if it is late.** Include an ownership roster, a backup admin, quality gates after refresh, a certified definition list, a support path, and meeting artifact rules. If you cannot publish that list, you are not in production.

2. **Run a hardening sprint on the renamed pilot.** Add no new pages unless they unblock hardening. Freeze scope theater and pay down the debt the rename skipped.

3. **Separate pilot workspaces from production apps by policy.** Require promotion and forbid copy-paste renames. Labels must match controls.

4. **Assign living owners and coverage.** Name KPI owners, workspace admins, and who has authority to change measures. This ties directly to [vacation coverage](/blog/workspace-has-no-vacation-coverage) and [orphaned tiles](/blog/kpi-owner-left-tile-still-named). Production means continuity.

5. **Add the second gate on refresh.** Green is not enough. Anything feeding executive decisions needs reconciliation and publish approval, as described in [refresh succeeded, data still wrong](/blog/refresh-succeeded-data-still-wrong).

6. **Rewrite the management cadence to use the product.** If the forum still runs on screenshots and side workbooks, you are demonstrating, not operating. Change the artifact rules.

7. **Retire pilot artifacts that never earned production.** Give temporary pages, measures, and extracts an end date, and follow [how to retire a dashboard](/blog/how-to-retire-a-dashboard).

8. **Tell the truth in steering.** “We scaled a pilot without hardening” is an uncomfortable sentence. It is also the only one that frees budget for foundations instead of more pages.

9. **Redefine success for the next ninety days.** Success means decisions running on a hardened model with owners, not another launch email. If the internal team is stuck maintaining the experiment, read [when to bring in help](/blog/when-to-bring-in-help).

## What good looks like

Production means a named product: a semantic model, apps, owners, quality gates, and a forum that uses them without heroic babysitting.

Pilots still exist, but they stay labeled Pilot. They have an end date and explicit promotion criteria, and they never reach broad executive distribution through a rename alone.

When leaders ask whether analytics is “live,” the answer points to operating controls, not to a workspace title that changed last spring.

## The rename test

Ask one question in the next steering meeting: if the original pilot builder left tomorrow, would the estate still meet its decision SLAs?

If the honest answer is no, you are not in production. You are in a permanent pilot with a confident label. Fix the label or fix the system, but do not keep living both lies.

## Budget the foundations explicitly

If the only funded work is “more reports,” the pilot debt never clears. Carve out a hardening budget line for ownership, coverage, quality gates, definition control, and cadence change.

Pages without foundations are inventory. Foundations without pages are unfinished. Production needs both, and the rename skipped the expensive half.

## A thirty-day hardening checklist

In week one, publish the exit criteria and an honest gap list. In week two, assign backup admins and measure owners. In week three, turn on post-refresh reconciliation for the executive dataset. In week four, change one forum’s artifact rule and retire two pilot pages that never earned distribution.

After thirty days you may still have a backlog, but you will no longer be pretending a renamed experiment is a finished operating system. That honesty comes before every later enhancement.

## Executive takeaway

Renaming a pilot to Production does not create a production analytics system. It creates a bigger blast radius for unfinished work.

Publish exit criteria, harden what you already shared, and keep true pilots small and time-boxed. Then the word Production means something a mid-market leadership team can underwrite.
